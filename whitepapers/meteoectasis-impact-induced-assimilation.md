# Meteoectasis: A Terminological Framework for Total Impact-Induced Assimilation and Dilatation in Planetary Accretion

> **What this paper is and is not.** This is a **terminology paper**, not a physics
> paper. It does not report new measurements, new simulations or a new impact
> model. What it proposes is a **name for a regime that already exists in the
> literature and is described there periphrastically**: the impact in which a
> smaller body is melted, thermally dilated and absorbed into a larger one until
> it loses its geochemical identity completely, while the target survives. The
> name is **meteoectasis**. The contribution is therefore a distinction — a
> boundary condition that separates three outcomes that are currently all called
> "accretion" — and a test for whether the distinction earns its keep. A reader
> looking for an energy balance calibrated against a hydrocode will not find one
> here, and the paper says so on the first page rather than the last.

## Abstract

The literature on planetary impacts describes **accretion** (the incorporation of
mass) and **impact-induced mixing** (the redistribution of material). Between them
there is a gap: no term describes the process in which an impactor of smaller mass
is *completely* melted, thermally dilated and assimilated into the larger body,
losing its geochemical and physical identity without destroying the target. This
paper proposes the term **meteoectasis** (from Greek *metéōron*, "celestial body",
and *éktasis*, "expansion, dilatation") for that regime. We define its boundary
conditions in terms of the **specific impact energy in relation to the latent heat
of fusion of the impactor**, distinguish it from *hit-and-run*, *accretionary
impact* and *catastrophic fragmentation*, and argue that the distinction is
operationally useful because it names the regime in which isotopic homogenisation
between two bodies becomes possible. The Earth–Moon system is used as the
motivating case, where the near-identity of oxygen isotopes between two supposedly
independent bodies is the anomaly that a term of this kind makes easier to state
precisely. The paper closes with the falsification test: what observation would
make us abandon the term.

## 1. Introduction

The study of planetary formation and the evolution of celestial bodies depends on
our ability to model collisions. Some terms are standard: *hit-and-run* for an
impact in which the impactor survives and escapes, *accretionary impact* for one in
which it is incorporated, *catastrophic disruption* for one in which the target is
destroyed.

Accretion, however, is a **quantity** word. It describes that mass moved from one
body to the other; it says nothing about the **state** of the material after the
event. A rock that sticks and a rock that vaporises and condenses on the other side
are both "accreted", and they are not the same event — not geochemically, not
thermally, not for the purpose of modelling the composition of the resulting body.

This is not pedantry. The composition of a planet after a collision depends on
whether the two bodies **exchanged material as distinguishable objects** or **were
dissolved into a common reservoir**. Modelling the first as if it were the second
produces a body whose isotopic budget cannot be explained; modelling the second as
if it were the first produces a body made of leftover fragments that are not
observed. The vocabulary currently available makes the two easy to confuse,
because both are called accretion.

The paper proposes a term for the second regime and, more importantly, a **set of
boundary conditions** that make the term checkable.

## 2. Definition

We define **meteoectasis** as the geophysical and astrophysical process by which an
impactor body is integrated into a larger target body through an impact of
**intermediate energy**, resulting in the complete fusion of the impactor, its
thermal dilatation (*éktasis*) and its dilution into the target's mantle or core,
**without the target being catastrophically fragmented**.

The definition has three parts and all three are necessary.

**2.1 It is a state, not a quantity.** The unit of the event is not "how much mass
moved" but "what happened to the material". The impactor ceases to exist as a
distinguishable body. This is what separates it from simple accretion.

**2.2 The dilatation is physical, not rhetorical.** *Éktasis* refers to the
volumetric expansion of material that has passed through the shock and been melted.
The material does not solidify into a discrete structure at the point of impact: it
flows and mixes with the target's magma ocean. The term names the expansion because
the expansion is what allows the mixing — a solid projectile embedded in the target
is an inclusion, a melted and expanded one is a constituent.

**2.3 The target survives.** If the target is destroyed, the event is fragmentation
and the question of assimilation does not arise. Meteoectasis is by definition a
process that **increases the target's mass and changes its composition**, not one
that ends it.

### Boundary conditions

| Regime | Specific impact energy | Outcome |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Hit-and-run** | Grazing geometry, energy deposited in a fraction of the impactor | Impactor or a large fragment **escapes** |
| **Catastrophic disruption** | Far above target's binding energy per unit mass | Target **fragmented**; a new family of bodies |
| **Accretionary impact** (simple) | Below impactor's latent heat of fusion | Impactor incorporated as a **distinguishable body**; composition is inherited, not mixed |
| **Meteoectasis** | **Above the impactor's latent heat of fusion, below the target's catastrophic threshold** | Impactor **melted, expanded and diluted**; composition becomes a mixture |

The operative quantity is the **specific impact energy** $Q_s$ compared with the
**latent heat of fusion** of the impactor $L_f$ (of order $4\times10^{5}$ J kg⁻¹ for
silicates, an order of magnitude less for ice) and against the **disruption
threshold** $Q_D^{*}$ of the target. The meteoectasis window is the interval

$$
L_f \;\ll\; Q_s \;\lesssim\; Q_D^{*}
$$

with the lower bound set by *complete* melting of the impactor material and the
upper bound set by survival of the target. Both bounds are approximate; what matters
is that the interval exists and is not empty for bodies of comparable size.

## 3. Energetics and thermodynamics

**3.1 Energy transfer.** The impactor's kinetic energy is dissipated in a shock
wave. Part of it melts the impactor, part of it melts a portion of the target, and
the remainder is radiated or carried away in ejecta. In the meteoectasis regime the
first term is sufficient to melt the impactor **entirely** — this is what
distinguishes it from a cratering event, where a small volume melts and the rest is
excavated.

**3.2 Thermal dilatation.** The melted material expands. The volumetric expansion is
small in absolute terms for silicates (of order a few per cent at melt temperatures
at ambient pressure) and it is not the point. The point is **rheological**: the
material loses its mechanical integrity and becomes part of a fluid.

**3.3 Homogenisation.** This is the consequence that makes the distinction matter.
Once both the impactor material and part of the target are in the same molten
reservoir, mixing proceeds on the timescale of the reservoir's overturn, not on the
timescale of solid-body dynamics. **Isotopic homogenisation between two bodies
becomes possible only in this regime.** In the simple accretion regime it is not:
two solid bodies that touch do not average their isotope ratios.

**3.4 Note on the lower bound.** The claim "sufficient to melt but insufficient to
fragment" is a useful first-order statement and it is not a precise criterion.
Melting is not instantaneous and not uniform: a shock melts the part it passes
through, and the portion of the impactor that is melted depends on the geometry of
its passage. The boundary conditions in §2 should be read as separating regimes, not
as classifying individual events. A given collision can be meteoectasis for part of
the impactor and simple accretion for the rest, and the honest description says so.

## 4. Applications in planetary modelling

Adopting the term gives modellers three named outcomes instead of one:

1. **Fragmentation events** — the target is destroyed; the question is what
   fragments survive.
2. **Rebound / escape events** — the impactor leaves, possibly modified; the target
   is largely unchanged in composition.
3. **Meteoectasis events** — the impactor is assimilated, diluting its composition
   into the target's and changing the target's bulk chemistry.

The classification is useful where a **compositional** question is being asked. It
is unnecessary where the question is simply how much mass arrived: there,
"accretion" remains the right word and the new term adds nothing.

### 4.1 The Earth–Moon case

The motivating example is the Moon-forming impact. A Mars-sized body (Theia)
collides with the proto-Earth; under the standard model, a large fraction of the
Moon's material derives from the impactor, and the impactor's mass is diluted into
the terrestrial mantle.

The anomaly is well known: **oxygen, titanium, tungsten and silicon isotope ratios
in lunar and terrestrial samples are nearly identical**, whereas planetary bodies
that formed independently in the solar nebula are expected to differ. Two bodies that
formed at different heliocentric distances should not be isotopically
indistinguishable.

There are several proposed resolutions — impactor formed at a similar distance,
post-impact equilibration through a vapour-rich disc, mixing in a common silicate
reservoir, tidal re-equilibration. Each is a mechanism. **What they have in common
is that all of them require the impactor material to be in a state in which
homogenisation is physically possible** — that is, they all require the impact to
have been meteoectasis rather than simple accretion. Naming the regime does not
choose between the mechanisms; it states the condition they all share, and thereby
makes clear that the argument is about *how* homogenisation happened and not
*whether* the event was capable of producing it.

## 5. What would make this term unnecessary

A terminology proposal should state its own defeat condition, or it is
unfalsifiable and therefore not a proposal but a preference.

The term should be abandoned if any of the following turns out to be true:

- **The regime is empty.** If, for every body size and impact geometry of interest,
  the interval $L_f \ll Q_s \lesssim Q_D^{*}$ is negligible — that is, if any impact
  energetic enough to melt the impactor also fragments the target — then
  meteoectasis does not occur and the term names nothing. This is an empirical
  question about the mass ratio and the energy scaling, and it is answerable.
- **The literature already distinguishes it.** If the existing vocabulary
  (*accretionary impact*, *merger*, *perfect merger*) is already used with this
  precise meaning by the community, a new term fragments rather than clarifies, and
  the correct action is to adopt the existing usage. Terminology should reduce
  ambiguity, not add synonyms.
- **The distinction carries no predictive weight.** If models in which the impactor
  is treated as an indistinguishable constituent and models in which it is treated as
  an inclusion produce the same observable predictions for every quantity of
  interest, the distinction is decorative. The paper's claim is that they differ in
  isotopic budgets and in the compositional stratification of the target, and that
  claim is checkable.

## 6. Conclusion

This paper introduces **meteoectasis** for the impact regime in which an impactor is
completely melted, thermally expanded and diluted into a larger target that
survives. The term fills a gap between *accretion*, which describes mass transfer,
and *impact-induced mixing*, which describes redistribution — neither of which names
the state the material reaches. The boundary conditions are stated in terms of
specific impact energy against the impactor's latent heat of fusion and the target's
disruption threshold, which makes the proposal checkable rather than merely
descriptive.

The paper does not claim that the physics is new. The physics is in the literature.
What is missing is a word for the state, and the consequence of the missing word is
visible: the Earth–Moon isotopic anomaly is discussed as if the difficulty were
mechanistic, when part of it is that the vocabulary does not separate "the impactor
was absorbed" from "the impactor was mixed". Restoring that separation is the
contribution, and the test of the contribution is whether the literature adopts the
word or replaces it with something better.
