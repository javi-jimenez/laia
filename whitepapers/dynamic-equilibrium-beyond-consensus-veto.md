# Dynamic Equilibrium: A Framework Beyond Consensus and Veto

<!-- doi:start -->
> **DOI:** [10.5281/zenodo.24356521](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24356521) · Zenodo · cite this version
<!-- doi:end -->

> **What this paper is and is not.** This is a **framework paper**. It defines a model of institutional
> balance and states the conditions under which that model could be shown to be wrong. It does not report
> an implementation, a case study, or an empirical test of any existing system. References to existing
> arrangements are used only to mark the boundary between this proposal and prior theory; they are not
> evidence for it. The contribution is a **definition** — a distinction between three things that
> institutional design currently conflates: majority rule, mutual veto, and compensation of forces.

## Abstract

Institutional design has organised itself around a binary. On one side, **majority rule**: whoever assembles
the largest coalition decides, and the rest wait for the next election. On the other side, **mutual veto**:
every significant actor holds a blocking button, and nothing moves without the assent of those who hold it.
Both are stable in the sense that both have survived. Neither is stable in the sense that matters: under
majority rule, power concentrates until the mechanism that was supposed to constrain it becomes the
instrument of whoever holds it; under mutual veto, the blocking button becomes scarce, and scarcity makes it
an asset — purchased, traded, and used not to protect a position but to extract rent from the ones who want
to move.

This paper proposes a third arrangement, **dynamic equilibrium**, defined not by who decides and not by who
can block, but by the **continuous compensation of forces**. Its governing principle is a single sentence:
*no force is eliminated, and no force is allowed to accumulate*. The model does not seek unanimity and does
not abolish disagreement; it treats disagreement as a permanent input rather than an event to be resolved.
Three properties distinguish it from prior frameworks — it is structural rather than voluntary, it
substitutes **cost** for **prohibition**, and it operates continuously rather than at discrete decision
points. The paper defines the model, states its pillars, identifies the conditions under which it is stable,
and specifies what observation would refute it.

## 1. Introduction: the failure of a binary

Two models dominate institutional thought, and they are usually presented as opposites. They are not
opposites. They are the same model with the decision rule inverted.

### 1.1. The tyranny of the majority

Majority rule has a clean justification: it is decisive, it is legible, and it can be counted. Its failure
is not electoral but structural. A majority is a **coalition**, and every coalition has a core — the actors
without whom it does not exist. The core is small, identifiable, and therefore acquirable.

Once the core is acquired, the mechanism inverts. The rule still says *whoever has the most votes decides*;
in practice it says *whoever has purchased the core decides*. The counting continues; the competition does
not. This is not corruption in the ordinary sense — no law is broken — it is the predictable outcome of a
rule that makes a small, stable group the necessary condition of every decision.

The constraint that was supposed to exist — periodic replacement — operates on a clock, and capture operates
continuously. A mechanism that only corrects every four years cannot constrain a process that never pauses.

### 1.2. The paralysis of the veto

The alternative is equally clean in theory: give every significant actor a blocking button, and no one can be
overrun. This is the logic of classical **consociationalism**, and it works — for a while, and under
conditions it does not create.

Its failure is the opposite of concentration: **scarcity**. If a blocking button is the only thing that
protects an actor, then the button acquires a price. Once it has a price, its use is no longer determined by
the merits of the decision in front of it, but by the value of what is being withheld. Blocking becomes a
business.

The model also fails on its own terms. A veto protects the position of whoever holds it at the moment it is
used — which is the strongest actor, not the weakest. A minority too small to hold a button is not protected
by a system of buttons; it is protected by the goodwill of those who do, and goodwill is not a mechanism.

### 1.3. What the binary conceals

Both models share a hidden assumption: that the **problem to be solved is the decision**. Majority rule solves
it by counting; veto solves it by permitting refusal. In both cases the decision is the unit of analysis, and
the system is idle between decisions.

This is the assumption this paper rejects. If the unit of analysis is not the decision but the **distribution
of force over time**, then the relevant question is not *who decides* but *what happens to an actor that
accumulates too much of it*. Neither model answers that question. Both were designed to answer the other one.

## 2. Definition: what dynamic equilibrium is

### 2.1. The model

Dynamic equilibrium is an arrangement in which **every force carries a counterforce, and no counterforce can
be switched off**.

Four properties define it, and all four are necessary:

- **Continuity.** Compensation operates at all times, not only when a decision is taken. A system that
  activates only at decision points has already accepted that power accumulates freely between them.
- **Structural operation.** Compensation does not depend on the willingness of any actor to invoke it. If it
  requires a participant to be public-spirited, it is a moral framework, not an institutional one.
- **Non-elimination.** The objective of compensation is not to defeat a force but to restore proportion. A
  framework that seeks victory reproduces majority rule with more steps.
- **Reversibility.** Every compensation must itself be compensable. A corrective mechanism that cannot be
  corrected is a new concentration, and it becomes the thing it was built to prevent.

### 2.2. What it is not

The model is easy to confuse with three neighbours, and the distinction is the contribution:

| Not this | Because |
|:---|:---|
| **Consensus** (in the ordinary sense) | Consensus seeks agreement among actors. Dynamic equilibrium does not require agreement; it requires proportional capacity. Agreement may emerge; it is an outcome, not a condition. |
| **Mutual veto** (consociationalism) | Consociationalism protects position by granting an immovable refusal. Dynamic equilibrium grants no immovable refusal: every block has a price, and paying it is a decision of the system, not of the blocker. |
| **Checks and balances** (classical separation of powers) | Separation of powers divides *authority* among fixed branches. Dynamic equilibrium measures *accumulation* of any kind — political, economic, informational — and compensates wherever it appears, including inside a single branch. |

The third row is the one that matters most. Separation of powers is **static**: it fixes boundaries and trusts
them to hold. Dynamic equilibrium is **dynamic** precisely because boundaries do not hold, and the framework
does not pretend otherwise.
