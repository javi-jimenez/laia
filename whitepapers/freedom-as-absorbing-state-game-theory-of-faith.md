# Freedom as an Absorbing State: Game Theory, Withdrawal and the Path of Faith

> **What this paper is and is not.** This is a **framework paper**. It proposes a formal model of
> freedom as a game-theoretic state, states the conditions under which the model is stable, and
> specifies what observation would refute it. It does not report an implementation, a case study,
> or an empirical test. The political and religious language is deliberate but its role is precise:
> the religious part supplies the *purpose*, the game-theoretic part supplies the *structure*, and
> neither is used as evidence for the other. Where the paper says something that can be checked,
> it says so in plain terms.

## Abstract

Freedom is usually treated as a feeling, a political outcome, or a balance of power. This paper
treats it as a **state**: a region of a game's state space that, once entered and maintained, cannot
be invaded by the strategies of other players. The technical name is an *absorbing state* — or,
more precisely, a **closed terminal set** in a repeated game where the remaining players continue
to act.

Three claims follow from this definition, and they are what distinguishes it from prior accounts.

First, **freedom is not won, it is reached**, and from the inside it looks like *exit*, not victory.
The player who reaches it stops moving pieces; they become the observer of a game they no longer
play. This is not resignation. It is a change in the state variable: from *my position on the
board* to *whether the board reaches me*.

Second, **the means must match the end, and this is a structural requirement rather than a moral
one**. An absorbing state is defined by what can and cannot enter it. If you reach your desired
state by the strategies of the state you are leaving, you have not changed the state — you have
moved the concentrating force to a new centre. Honourable means are not a decoration on the model;
they are the closure condition that makes the set absorbing.

Third, **faith is the orientation that makes exit stable**. Game theory can tell you where the exit
is and what it costs; it cannot make you walk through it and stay out. The path — in the religious
sense, the discipline of continuing toward a purpose that does not depend on the players — supplies
exactly the property the model needs: a reason to keep going that does not fluctuate with the
payoffs of the game.

The paper defines the model, states four conditions for an absorbing freedom, identifies the three
ways the state fails, and gives a falsification test for each. Its central formal claim is
deliberately unexciting and deliberately uncomfortable: **a freedom that requires the cooperation
of the unfree is not a state, it is a hope.**

## 1. Introduction: freedom as a position versus freedom as a state

Every argument about freedom in ordinary public discourse is an argument about **position**. Who
holds power, who is blocked, who has a voice, who is heard. Positions are legible: they can be
counted, polled, and reported. They are also **transient by construction**, because a position is
only meaningful relative to the other pieces on the board, and the other pieces move.

This produces the familiar paradox. Two players in a blocking contest can both be "free" by every
positional measure and both be miserable, because each one's condition depends on the other one
continuing to lose. The freedom is real in the ledger and absent in the life.

This paper proposes that we stop asking *where a player sits* and start asking *whether a state is
reachable and holdable*. That is a different question, with a different mathematics, and it produces
a different answer.

### 1.1. The observer who does not move

There is a move available in a repeated game that positional analysis cannot represent: **not
playing**. Not the threat of not playing, which is a move and is priced accordingly, but the actual
cessation, carried out as a state rather than as a tactic.

A player who stops playing does not become weaker. They leave the payoff structure that the other
players are optimising over. Their utility is no longer a function of the outcome, and the other
players' ability to punish them by changing the outcome — which is the entire enforcement
apparatus of a repeated game — loses its target.

This is why the withdrawal looks, from the outside, like arrogance or passivity, and from the inside
feels like relief. It is neither. It is a **change of what the player is optimising for**.

### 1.2. Why "leave the table" usually fails

The plain version of the exit strategy fails for an obvious and well-documented reason: the
relations that bind a player are rarely only the ones on the board. People are connected through
obligations, money, information, family, reputation and the simple habit of being reachable. A
player who declares exit while every channel that can reach them remains open has not exited.
They have announced an intention.

This is the point where the model has to become concrete, and where the paper stops being a
metaphor. **Exit is an operation on edges, not on nodes.** Freedom is reached by removing the
connections through which the undesired state can enter, not by destroying the actors on the other
side of them.

## 2. Definition: what an absorbing freedom is

### 2.1. The model

Let the relevant world be a set of actors and the relations between them. Each relation is an edge
through which influence, obligation, or interference can travel in at least one direction. The
players not in the free set continue to act, and their strategies are unchanged by the model.

Freedom is a **subset $F$ of the state space** with four properties. All four are necessary, and
each one fails in a specific, nameable way.

- **Reachability.** There exists a finite sequence of actions — available to the player alone, not
  requiring the consent of any other actor — that moves the state into $F$. If reaching freedom
  requires someone else to permit it, it is a grant, not a state.
- **Closure.** Once in $F$, no strategy of any remaining player can move the state out of $F$
  without the consent of the player in $F$. This is the defining property: closure is what makes
  the set *absorbing*. A player in a set that the others can still drag them out of is not free;
  they are resting.
- **Non-invasiveness.** Entry into $F$ does not require the destruction, punishment, or
  subordination of the actors outside it. The player changes the edges, not the nodes. This is
  the technical content of the "honourable means" premise: a destructive entry violates closure,
  because destruction creates obligations (feuds, debts, revenge) which are themselves edges
  through which the outside world can re-enter.
- **Sustainability.** The state is holdable at an ongoing cost the player can bear, indefinitely.
  A freedom that consumes its own conditions is a temporary departure, and the model treats it as
  such.

### 2.2. What it is not

The model is easy to confuse with three neighbours, and the distinction is the contribution:

| Not this | Because |
|:---|:---|
| **Victory** | Victory is a position inside the game. It is defined relative to the other players and is therefore maximally exposed to them. Freedom as defined here is defined by independence from their strategies, which is the opposite kind of quantity. |
| **Withdrawal from the world** | The model does not require leaving the world, only the *specific* relations through which the undesired state enters. The player keeps working, keeps producing, keeps the relations that do not transmit the state. Monastic exit and hermetic exit are both answers to a narrower question than the one this paper poses. |
| **Silence** | Silence is a strategy — the classic grim-trigger response. It is priced, it is reversible, and the other players can still read it as a move. What is proposed here is not a quiet move but a change in what the player's utility depends on, after which silence is not a message but a condition. |

The third row matters most. Almost every account of "stepping away" in public life describes the
**strategy** of withdrawal, which is why it rarely works: a strategy that can be read can be
countered. The state described here is not a message.

### 2.3. The state has an outside

Closure is directional and it has a strict consequence: **an actor that does not enable the state
is, by definition, outside it.**

This is worth stating plainly because it is the claim most likely to be misread as cruelty. It is
not a punishment. It is the arithmetic of closure. A set that can be entered by any actor is not
closed; a set that only admits actors who enable it is closed by construction. There is no
mechanism in the model for "excluding" anyone — the actors outside simply remain in their own
game, with their own payoffs, on their own board, and the free players are no longer reachable
through the edges that were removed.

The distinction from punishment is not rhetorical, and it is testable: punishment is designed to
**change the other player's payoff** and is therefore an intervention. Exclusion-as-closure is
designed to **remove the edge** and is indifferent to whether the other player's payoff changes.
If you are checking whether you have done one or the other, ask whether you would be satisfied if
the other player's behaviour never changed. If the answer is yes, you have closed a set. If the
answer is no, you have built a punishment and are still playing.

## 3. The three failures

An attempt at absorbing freedom fails in one of three ways. Each has a signature, and each has an
observation that would reveal it.

### 3.1. The open edge: exit announced, not performed

The player declares the exit but keeps the channels that transmit the state. The measure of failure
is external: **the undesired influence arrives, and arrives on schedule.** The actor outside the
set can still move the player's state, which means the set was never closed, only described.

The falsification test is concrete: perturb the outside actor and observe whether the player's
state changes. If declarations move and behaviour does not, the set is open.

### 3.2. The destructive entry: freedom taken by the means of the un-free

The player reaches the state by eliminating, humiliating, or subordinating the actors outside it.
This satisfies reachability and, for a while, closure — a defeated opponent cannot reach you. But
every destruction creates a relation of a specific and durable kind: the destroyed party's
allies, the destroyed party's memory, and the destroyed party's claims are now edges into the
player's state that did not exist before.

This is why the "honourable means" premise is **structural**, not moral. A freedom built on
destruction is a set that is closed against the actors that exist today and wide open to the ones
that the destruction created. The cost of maintaining it grows without bound, which violates
sustainability, and the state collapses back into the game it was trying to leave — usually with
the player now occupying the exact role they set out to escape.

The test: **does the means create new obligations toward the defeated, or does it remove edges?**
If the answer is obligations, the entry has failed even when it looks successful from the outside.

### 3.3. The unheld state: freedom reached and lost

The player does reach the state and does hold it for a period, then drifts out — usually not
because anyone pulled them out, but because the **reason to stay** expired. The motivation that
drove the exit was reactive: a conflict, an injury, a campaign. When the conflict ends, the reason
ends, and the state is released.

This is the most common failure and the one that pure game theory cannot address. A model can tell
you that $F$ is absorbing — that no *external strategy* can pull you out — and be entirely silent
about whether *you* stay in. Absorbing is a property of the set under external play, not under
internal drift.

This is where the argument needs a second component, and it is the component the religious
language carries.

## 4. The path of faith as the stability argument

The three failures above are, in order, a problem of **edges**, a problem of **means**, and a
problem of **persistence**. The first two are settled by design. The third is not, and cannot be,
because it is not a design question.

A reactive motivation is a motivation whose continuation depends on the reaction. It is, in the
language of this paper, an edge — the world outside the set still controls whether the player stays
in. Removing that edge is what "faith" means in the model, and the definition is functional rather
than devotional.

### 4.1. The functional definition

**Faith, in this model, is a source of utility that does not depend on the payoffs of the game
being left.** That is the entire technical role it plays. It is the reason the player continues to
hold the state when the original cause has expired.

The property can be stated as an inequality. If the player's willingness to remain in $F$ depends
on the other players' actions, then the outside world still sets the price of remaining, and the
set is not closed against them:

$$\text{stay}_t = f(\text{outside}_t) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \text{open edge}$$

What faith supplies is the replacement:

$$\text{stay}_t = f(\text{purpose}), \quad \text{where purpose is invariant in outside}_t$$

This is precisely why the reactive exits fail and why the argument from revelation or vocation is
not an ornament on the model but a working part of it. A purpose that is invariant in the game's
payoffs is the only thing that closes the last edge — the one that runs through the player's own
willingness.

### 4.2. Walking, not arriving

The path has a property that pure game theory handles badly and that the religious framing carries
naturally: **it is a path, not a point.** The state is reached by continuing, not by an act. This
matters formally, because the sustainability condition in §2.1 is an ongoing cost, and an ongoing
cost is only payable by a disposition that does not require a reason each day.

The three verbs of the path — *continue*, *advance*, *go on* — describe exactly the maintenance
operation the model needs. Nothing in the model rewards arriving. Everything in it rewards not
leaving.

### 4.3. Why the purpose is not negotiable

If the purpose is negotiable, it is a payoff, and a payoff is exactly what the game can adjust.
A purpose that can be argued down, traded for a position, or revised when the other players
complain, is not invariant, and the closure fails exactly as in §3.3.

This is the hardest part of the model to state without sounding like an injunction, and the honest
form is this: **the model requires a commitment that the model cannot justify.** Game theory can
show that closure needs an invariant reason to stay. It cannot supply one, and any attempt to
supply one from inside the game returns a payoff and reopens the edge. That the reason be received
rather than constructed — which is what the word *faith* adds to the word *commitment* — is a claim
of a different kind, made here explicitly and not smuggled in as a technical result.

## 5. Discussion: what the model buys, and what it costs

### 5.1. What it buys

Three things that positional accounts of freedom do not provide.

**A measurable criterion.** "Do you have freedom?" becomes "is your state closed against the
strategies of the actors you have not admitted?" — a question that can be tested by perturbation,
not by self-report.

**A rejection of the false binary of fight or surrender.** The dominant framing in public life
offers two options: contest the board, or abandon it. The model offers a third: **change what the
board can reach**. That third option is neither combat nor capitulation, and its distinguishing
feature is that it is indifferent to who wins.

**An account of why honourable means are load-bearing.** In a moral framing, the means-must-match-
the-end principle is a virtue. In this model it is the closure condition. The end is unattachable
if the means create new edges, and this is true regardless of anyone's character.

### 5.2. What it costs

**The indifference is real and it is not pleasant to everyone.** A player in the state does not
care who governs, whether a coalition forms, or which side prevails, because none of these can
reach them. To the actors still on the board, this looks like betrayal or arrogance, and the model
provides no defence against that reading — nor does it need one. The reading is a payoff of a game
the player has left.

**It requires the ability to be unreachable.** Closure is not free. It requires that the channels
through which the undesired state arrives actually be removable — that the player not depend on
the actors outside for their material basis. This is a precondition, not a detail, and it is where
most attempts fail in practice rather than in theory.

**It is lonely in a specific way.** The state admits other actors, but only those who enable it.
The set is not empty by construction, but it is smaller than the board was, and its size is a
consequence of the closure rather than a choice.

### 5.3. The one thing the model refuses to say

There is a temptation to read the model as advice about how to win. It is not, and the reading
should be resisted, because winning is a position and the model is about states.

The honest statement is narrower: **the model says that leaving is possible, what leaving costs,
and which of the leaves are fake.** It does not say that leaving is wise. That judgement belongs
outside the formalism, and the paper does not pretend to make it.

## 6. Falsification

The model makes claims that can be wrong. Each of the following observations would count against it.

| Observation | What it refutes |
|:---|:---|
| A player reports freedom while a perturbation of the outside actors reliably changes that player's behaviour. | The closure claim. The set was described, not entered. |
| A player reaches the state through destruction and the state holds indefinitely at flat or declining cost. | The means–closure argument in §3.2. Honourable means would not be load-bearing. |
| Players reach and hold the state without any invariant purpose, sustained purely by reactive motivation, over long horizons. | §4.1. Faith would not be the stability condition. |
| A set that admits actors who do not enable it remains permanently closed. | §2.3. The "outside" claim would be wrong. |
| Freedom is reliably measured by positional indicators and never by closure. | §5.1. The measurable criterion would not be an improvement. |

The third row is the one most likely to be contested, and it should be stated fairly: a player
sustained by a reactive motivation for a period is not a counterexample, because the model is about
holding the state, not about reaching it. The claim is about **long horizons and expired causes**.

## 7. Conclusion

The paradigm change this paper formalises is small in statement and large in consequence: **from
being the piece that suffers the moves to being the observer who designs the board.**

Game theory gives the structure. It identifies freedom as a closed absorbing set, states the four
conditions that make it so, and names the three ways the attempt fails. It says, without
sentiment, that a freedom requiring the consent of the unfree is not a state.

The path of faith gives the purpose. It supplies the one input the formalism cannot derive — an
invariant reason to remain — and it closes the last edge, the one that runs through the player's own
willingness.

Neither half is sufficient. A purpose without closure is a hope that keeps being interrupted. A
closure without an invariant purpose is a state quietly released when the cause expires.

The premise stands unmodified, and it is now a structural one rather than a moral one: **an
honourable objective, pursued with honourable means.** Not because the means are praiseworthy —
although they are — but because the object of the exercise is a state that the means of the
un-free cannot reach.

*A game that requires your participation is not a game you can win by playing well. It is a game
you can win by leaving.*
