# The Lux Equation: Measuring the Economic Return of Gratitude and Congregation, and What It Cannot Prove

> **What this paper is and is not.** The original article behind this paper made a stronger claim than the
> evidence supports: that a measurable energy surplus *demonstrates* the existence of God. This paper keeps the
> measurable half and separates it explicitly from the unmeasurable half. It argues that **gratitude and
> religious congregation produce large, quantifiable economic effects through health outcomes and social
> capital**, and that **the economic argument cannot settle a metaphysical question** — not because the question
> is unimportant, but because economics is the wrong instrument for it. The separation is the paper's
> contribution; a reader looking for a proof of God will not find one here, and the paper says so at the top
> rather than at the bottom.

## Abstract

Claims about the value of gratitude and religious practice are usually argued in one of two registers: as
pious assertion, or as dismissible anecdote. This paper argues there is a **third register that is defensible**:
both practices are associated, in large prospective cohort studies and meta-analyses, with measurable
differences in mortality, depression and self-reported wellbeing, and those differences have a monetisable
shadow in health-system cost and labour productivity.

Two literatures are used. On **congregation**, prospective cohorts associate regular religious-service
attendance with lower depression and lower mortality, after adjustment for confounders. On **gratitude**,
randomised interventions with active controls show small but replicated effects on wellbeing, with the honest
caveat that effect sizes are small and publication bias is a live concern.

The paper then does the part usually skipped: it **converts those effects into money with the assumptions
visible**, and it reports the result as a range whose width is dominated by the assumptions rather than by the
evidence. Finally it addresses the original claim directly: **an economic surplus is consistent with a
metaphysical source and does not establish one**, and the paper explains the specific logical error involved —
treating a proxy measurement as a measurement of the thing it is a proxy for.

## 1. The problem with how this is usually argued

The original article posed a sharp question: how do you show the Sacred to someone who only accepts the
scientific method and the balance of results? The answer offered was the **Law of Energy Summation** — when
you give thanks, your energy rises, and since giving normally depletes, the surplus must come from a Universal
Source.

The question is good. **The inference does not hold**, and it fails for a specific and instructive reason.

The observation — that gratitude is associated with improved wellbeing — is empirically supported. The
conclusion — that a surplus implies an external source — requires a premise the observation cannot supply: that
the wellbeing effect has no natural mechanism. That premise is false or at least unnecessary: gratitude
interventions plausibly work through attention, rumination, social bonding and stress physiology, all of which
are natural mechanisms, and all of which are exactly what the studies measure.

So the argument as written proves less than it claims. **But it is aiming at something real**, and the real
thing is worth stating properly: *the effects are large enough to matter economically*. That is a claim about
magnitude, and magnitude is measurable. This paper measures it.

## 2. What the evidence actually shows

### 2.1. Congregation

Large prospective cohort studies, adjusted for confounders, report associations between regular
religious-service attendance and lower depression and lower mortality:

| Study | Design | Finding |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Li, Okereke, Chang & Kawachi (2016), *Annals of Behavioral Medicine* | Prospective cohort, women | Attendance associated with **lower depression** |
| Lindström & Pirouzifard (2023), *SSM – Population Health* | Population-based prospective cohort, southern Sweden | Attendance associated with **lower mortality** |
| Darviri et al. (2016), *Psychology & Health* | Retrospective cohort, 1,519 rural citizens, Greece | Religiosity as a **predictor of mortality** |
| Garssen et al. (2020), *Int. J. for the Psychology of Religion* | Meta-analysis of **longitudinal** studies | Spirituality/religion associated with **better mental health** |

**The honest caveat, which belongs here rather than in a footnote:** these are observational associations.
Attendance correlates with health-seeking behaviour, social support, lower risky behaviour and higher
socioeconomic position. Studies adjust for these, and adjustment is never complete. The direction of causality
is plausible in both directions — healthier people attend more, and attendees are healthier.
**Trials of random assignment to congregation do not exist and will not exist.**

### 2.2. Gratitude

Randomised interventions with active control conditions produce small, replicated improvements in wellbeing.
The most recent meta-analysis of randomised studies (Kirca, Malouff & Meynadier, 2023, *International Journal
of Applied Positive Psychology*) supports a real effect.

**The honest caveat:** effect sizes are **small** (in the range typical of brief psychological interventions),
follow-up is short, and the field has a publication-bias problem. A small effect measured badly is not the same
as a large effect measured well.

### 2.3. Social capital

The mechanism most likely to carry the health effect is not devotion as such but **congregation as repeated
co-present social interaction** — the thing Putnam measured when he documented the decline of associational
life. This matters for the argument: if the mechanism is social capital rather than belief, then the effect
should be reproducible by any institution that produces durable social capital, **and that prediction is
testable**. It has not been tested here, and the paper flags it as the strongest available test of its own
mechanism claim.

## 3. Converting effects into money — with the assumptions visible

Health effects have a monetisable shadow through two channels: **cost avoided** (treatment not delivered)
and **output preserved** (productive years not lost). The conversion requires assumptions, and the assumptions
dominate the result. They are therefore listed as the substance of the calculation, not as its apparatus.

### 3.1. The model

For a population of size *N* with a prevalence of a given condition *p*, and a relative reduction *r*
attributable to the practice:

$$\text{Cost avoided} = N \cdot p \cdot r \cdot C_{\text{case}}$$

where $C_{\text{case}}$ is the average annual cost per case (direct treatment plus monetised lost output).

### 3.2. Worked example, stated as an example

Using population and cost figures for a high-income country of **N = 47 million** (Spain's order of
magnitude), depression prevalence **p = 5 %** (a defensible mid-range point prevalence), average annual cost per
case **C = €3,000** (treatment plus productivity loss, also mid-range), and a relative reduction of
**r = 10 %** — deliberately *below* the associations reported in §2.1, because an observational association
should never be spent at face value:

$$\text{Cost avoided} = 47\times10^{6} \cdot 0.05 \cdot 0.10 \cdot 3{,}000 \approx €705 \text{ million/year}$$

**The number is not the point. The sensitivity is.** Holding the population and prevalence fixed:

| Relative reduction *r* | Cost avoided per year |
|:---|---:|
| 5 % | €353 million |
| 10 % | €705 million |
| 20 % | €1,410 million |

**A factor of four between the low and high case, driven entirely by one parameter nobody has measured
causally.** This is why the paper reports a range and refuses a point estimate: presenting €705 million as
"the" figure would be exactly the kind of unearned precision the original article was criticised for.

### 3.3. What this does and does not establish

It establishes that **the order of magnitude is economically meaningful**: hundreds of millions per year for a
single mid-sized country, on a single condition, with the effect deliberately discounted. It does not
establish causation, and it does not establish that money spent promoting the practice would return the
amount — the association is between practice and outcome in people who already practise, which is a different
population from one subjected to an intervention.

## 4. The step that cannot be taken

Now the part that matters most, and the reason this paper exists.

An economic surplus is **consistent with** the original article's conclusion and **does not establish it**.
The distinction is not pedantry; it is the difference between a measurement and a metaphor.

The error has a name in measurement theory: **treating a proxy as the thing it proxies**. Wellbeing is
measured by questionnaires; cost is modelled from prevalence and unit cost; the practice is measured by
attendance records. Each link in the chain is a proxy, and a proxy can be excellent and still not be the
referent.

**An economic effect tells you the practice is valuable. It does not tell you why.** A revealed value of
hundreds of millions of euros says something true and important about human flourishing under this practice. It
is silent — necessarily silent, not coyly silent — on whether the practice connects to a source outside the
natural order.

**The paper's position, stated plainly:** the measurable half is real and should be argued with numbers. The
unmeasurable half is not refuted by that, and it is not established by it either. Conflating them weakens the
first half — which is the half that can actually persuade anyone who does not already agree.

There is also a strategic argument, which is the practical one: **an argument that overclaims is discarded
entirely when the overclaim is found.** An argument that claims only what it can show survives being checked,
and being checkable is the only property that makes a claim useful to someone who disagrees.

## 5. What this paper does not claim

- **Not that attendance causes health.** Observational association, adjusted, confounded, and stated as such.
- **Not that the €705 million is real money available.** It is cost avoided under stated assumptions, not a
  budget line.
- **Not that promoting the practice would produce the effect.** The studied population is self-selected.
- **Not that gratitude interventions are potent.** The effects are small; the paper says so twice because it
  is the weakest link.
- **Not that any of this settles a metaphysical question**, in either direction.

## 6. Conclusion

The Lux Equation, restated so that it can survive examination:

> **Gratitude and congregation are associated with measurable health outcomes, and those outcomes have an
> economic value in the hundreds of millions per year for a single mid-sized country, on one condition, at a
> deliberately discounted effect size. The value is real. What it implies about its own source is not
> measurable by this instrument, and claiming otherwise would cost the argument the credibility it has earned.**

The original article asked how to show the Sacred to someone who only trusts the scientific method and the
balance of results. This paper's answer is narrower and, I think, more useful: **you cannot. But you can show
them a number that is large, checkable, and honestly bounded — and then let them ask what it means.** The
question is theirs to answer. Insisting on answering it for them is how the number gets thrown away.
