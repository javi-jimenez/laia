# Defence Is Cheaper Than Attack: A Cost Accounting of Offensive Versus Defensive Posture

> **Status: conjecture, with a model.** The original working note asserted a figure of "500 trillion" — which,
> in Spanish notation, would be **16.7 times US GDP** and therefore impossible. The corrected figure, in
> standard notation, is **500 billion (US$ 0.5 trillion ≈ 1.7 % of GDP)**. This paper builds that figure from
> five components with every assumption visible, reports the result as a range, and states plainly that
> **the result is sensitive to assumptions that nobody has measured**. It is a framework for arguing, not a
> finding.

## Abstract

Defence spending is normally evaluated on capability — what it buys, whether it deters. It is rarely evaluated
on **relative return**: what the same money would have produced in a defensive posture versus an offensive one.

This paper proposes that the two postures are not symmetric in cost, and that the asymmetry is large enough to
dominate most capability arguments. It constructs a five-component cost model — multiplier differential on the
reallocated share, direct cost of offensive operations, debt service, trade disruption, and reconstruction
commitments — and applies it to a US-scale economy. The central estimate is approximately **US$ 500 billion per
year (≈ 1.7 % of GDP)**, with a sensitivity range of **450–600 billion** driven almost entirely by one
parameter: how much of the budget is actually reallocable.

The conclusion is deliberately narrow: **the claim is not that offence never buys security.** It is that
offence is priced as if it were the only instrument that produces security, when a substantial part of what it
buys can be bought more cheaply by reinforcement — and the difference is large enough to be a policy question
rather than a rounding error.

## 1. Why this is usually not asked

Defence budgets are argued in one of two registers. Either **capability** ("this platform is necessary because
the threat requires it") or **affordability** ("we cannot afford it / we cannot afford not to"). Neither
register asks the comparative question: *if this money were spent differently — still on security, but on
reinforcement rather than on reach — what would the return be?*

There are three reasons the question is suppressed, and they are worth naming because they explain why a
seemingly obvious accounting is not done.

**The costs are in different ledgers.** Procurement is in one budget line, veterans' care in another,
reconstruction in a supplementary estimate, trade disruption in the private sector, and debt service in a
finance ministry. **No single account sees the total**, so no single account is asked to defend it.

**Benefits are contested and costs are deferred.** The benefit of offence is a counterfactual (a war that did
not happen). The cost of offence arrives years later. A decision-maker who bears neither has no incentive to
count.

**Offence is politically legible; reinforcement is not.** A ship is a headline; hardened infrastructure is a
report. This is a political economy problem, not an economic one, and it is the real obstacle to the argument
in this paper.

## 2. The distinction being drawn

The paper uses a narrow and operational distinction:

- **Defensive posture (D):** expenditure that raises the cost of attacking you — territorial defence, hardened
  infrastructure, resilience, civil protection, deterrence by denial, redundancy.
- **Offensive posture (O):** expenditure that projects force into someone else's territory or airspace —
  long-range strike, expeditionary operations, munitions consumed offensively, occupation, and the
  reconstruction and compensation obligations that follow.

**These are not moral categories.** Both are instruments of security, and a state may legitimately need both.
The claim is purely about **relative return**, and it is stated so that it can be wrong: *for a given level of
security, D costs less than O by a margin that is economically material.*

**Two things the model deliberately excludes**, because including them would make it look like advocacy: the
value of any strategic objective achieved by offensive action, and the value of deterrence produced by
offensive capability. Excluding them biases the result **in favour** of this paper's conclusion, which is why
they are named here and revisited in §6.

## 3. The model

Units are **billions of US dollars (10⁹)** throughout. "500" means 500 billion. GDP is taken at **30,000**
billion (US$ 30 trillion, the order of magnitude of the US economy).

Five components:

### 3.1. Multiplier differential on the reallocated share

Public spending does not produce the same output per dollar. Defence procurement has a fiscal multiplier
commonly estimated in the **0.7–0.9** range: a large share is imported equipment and specialised capital with
limited domestic spillover. Domestic public investment — infrastructure, education, research, health — is
commonly estimated in the **1.2–1.4** range.

Taking the midpoint differential of **0.5** (i.e. 1.3 − 0.8) and a **reallocable share of 400 billion** — the
part of the budget that is genuinely discretionary between the two postures rather than fixed in pay and
existing commitments:

$$400 \times 0.5 = \mathbf{200}$$

This is the largest and most contested component. It assumes that 400 billion of the budget could in principle
be redirected without reducing security, which is exactly the thing the paper is arguing for. **The component
is therefore circular if used alone**, and is presented as part of a total rather than as a standalone result.

### 3.2. Direct cost of offensive operations

Munitions consumed, deployment, force rotation, replacement of expended stocks: **180**.

### 3.3. Debt service attributable

Interest on the portion of public debt incurred for offensive operations: **60**.

### 3.4. Trade and risk-premium disruption

Insurance, freight, energy premia and the trade volume not conducted because of elevated risk: **70**.

### 3.5. Reconstruction and commitments

Rebuilding, compensation, and multi-year obligations incurred by offensive action: **40**.

### 3.6. Result

| Component | Billions US$ |
|:---|---:|
| 1. Multiplier differential (400 × 0.5) | 200 |
| 2. Direct offensive operations | 180 |
| 3. Debt service attributable | 60 |
| 4. Trade and risk-premium disruption | 70 |
| 5. Reconstruction and commitments | 40 |
| **Total** | **550** |

As a share of GDP: **550 / 30,000 ≈ 1.83 %**.

**Central estimate: US$ 500 billion per year, ≈ 1.7 % of GDP**, rounded **down** from the model's 550 to
acknowledge that components 2–5 are the author's estimates rather than measured quantities.

## 4. Sensitivity — and why it is the most important section

The result moves by a factor of 1.3 across the plausible range of the only truly uncertain parameter:

| Reallocable share | Total (billions) | % of GDP |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 200 | 450 | 1.50 % |
| 300 | 500 | 1.67 % |
| 400 | 550 | 1.83 % |
| 500 | 600 | 2.00 % |

**This is a range of 450–600 billion, and the width comes from assumptions, not from evidence.** A paper that
reported 500 with three significant figures would be manufacturing precision that does not exist. The
defensible claim is: *of the order of half a trillion dollars a year, roughly one to two percent of GDP, on
assumptions that can be argued about.*

## 5. The positive-sum claim, and its limit

The stronger version of the argument — the one in the original note — is that **every country and region runs a
positive balance from investing internally rather than in attacking one another**, because trade gains are
positive-sum while territorial gains are zero- or negative-sum once the cost of taking and holding is counted.

That claim has real support in the broad sense: trade raises aggregate output, and war destroys capital faster
than it captures it. **But it is not universally true**, and the paper will not pretend otherwise. Two
counter-examples are inherent, not accidental:

- **Positional goods.** If what is contested is a chokepoint, an exclusive resource or a route, the gain from
  control can exceed the cost of taking it — for the taker, at least, and for a time. This is precisely why
  chokepoints generate conflict, and it is the subject of the companion paper on trade arteries.
- **Security dilemmas.** Defensive reinforcement by one party can be read as offensive preparation by another,
  producing arms races that neither wanted. The model prices the posture, not the reaction to it.

So the honest form of the positive-sum claim is conditional: **it holds where what is at stake is production
rather than position.** That is a large and important class of situations, and it is not all of them.

## 6. Objections and what would refute this

**"Defence spending is not discretionary at 400 billion."** Probably correct, and it is why §3.1 is flagged as
circular. The model's floor — components 2–5 alone, **350 billion** — does not depend on that assumption at
all, and is still material. **A reader who rejects the multiplier argument entirely should keep the 350.**

**"You have ignored the strategic objectives offence achieves."** Deliberately, and stated in §2. If an
offensive campaign secures an objective worth more than 500 billion per year, the posture pays. The paper's
claim is that this is **rarely demonstrated at that price**, not that it is impossible.

**"Multipliers are contested."** Entirely. Reasonable estimates for defence multipliers span 0.6–1.1. This is
why the differential is one component among five and why the result is a range.

**"This is an argument for disarmament."** It is not, and the distinction matters: the model recommends
**reinforcement over reach**, which is a redistribution *within* security spending, not a reduction of it.
Defensive posture is not cheap. It is cheaper.

**What would falsify the central claim:** a state that sustained an offensive posture for a decade at lower
total cost — including debt service, trade disruption and reconstruction — than a comparable state sustained a
defensive one at equivalent security. The paper has not found such a case, and does not claim to have searched
exhaustively.

## 7. Where NEPTUNO enters — and the honest boundary

This paper is written within a company that runs a framework called **NEPTUNO**, whose three-layer model is
described in WP 16. That framework's ordering claim — *projection requires immunisation, immunisation requires
governance* — is structurally the same argument as this paper's: **reinforce the base before you reach beyond
it.**

**The parallel is offered as an analogy, not as evidence.** A resilience framework for organisations does not
demonstrate anything about defence economics; it is a different domain, a different scale and a different
actor. What the two share is a way of reasoning about the order in which expenditure should be committed — and
that reasoning is what is being proposed here, not any empirical transfer of results.

Stating this boundary is not modesty; it is the condition under which the paper can be used. A defence
economist who is told that a company's framework validates their field will correctly stop reading. One who is
told the structural logic is the same, and the domains are different, can evaluate the logic on its merits.

## 8. Conclusion

The claim, at the strength it can survive:

> **Offensive posture carries costs that are distributed across ledgers nobody totals: multiplier loss, direct
> operations, debt service, trade disruption and reconstruction. On a US-scale economy those costs are of the
> order of half a trillion dollars a year — one to two percent of GDP — and the estimate is dominated by
> assumptions rather than by measurements. The comparative case for reinforcement over reach is therefore
> strong enough to be a policy question, and not settled enough to be a policy conclusion.**

That is a weaker claim than the original note. It is also the only version that survives being checked, and
being checkable is what makes it usable by someone who disagrees.
